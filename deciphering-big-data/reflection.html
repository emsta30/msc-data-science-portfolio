Reflective learning journey

To reflect on my experiences throughout this module, I have used Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper's (2001) 'What? So what? Now what?' framework, which provides a structure for considering the work I completed, what I learned from the challenges I encountered and how I intend to apply that learning in the future.

What? Understanding my learning experience

I began this module with a background in marketing and practical experience of analysing business information, but relatively limited experience of programming and relational database development. Although I was comfortable interpreting data and presenting findings, the technical processes involved in extracting, cleaning and modelling datasets were less familiar.

The main collaborative project centred on the development of an AI-assisted database analytics proposal for a hypothetical healthcare organisation, using a dataset comprising 10,000 hospital encounters and incorporating demographic, clinical and operational information. Within the group, I was responsible for investigating the potential applications of artificial intelligence, particularly how it could support data profiling, inform the identification of an appropriate database schema and assist with Python code generation.

I used Google Colab to execute Python code, with Google Gemini assisting with parts of the programming and analysis. The group produced a proposed relational database design, validation approach and dashboard concept. I subsequently developed this work further through an individual implementation using SQLite and R Shiny.

So what? Critical analysis of my learning

One of my main challenges was developing the confidence to work with unfamiliar programming tools. AI assistance made Python more accessible because I could describe the analysis I wanted to perform and receive suggested code. Initially, this reduced the technical barrier to exploring the dataset. However, I recognised that successfully executing code was not equivalent to understanding whether its outputs were correct.

A significant learning moment occurred when AI suggested treating patient identifiers as reliable keys, despite inconsistencies in the associated demographic information. It also proposed resolving conflicting values by selecting the earliest or most frequent observation. Although these suggestions appeared reasonable, neither method established which information was accurate. This challenged my initial perception of AI as a dependable shortcut through unfamiliar technical processes.

Using Python to examine the data revealed that 47.5% of encounters contained checkout dates preceding check-in dates. Further checks identified discrepancies in recorded lengths of stay and conflicting demographic information associated with repeated patient identifiers. These findings demonstrated that a dataset can contain no conventional missing values while still having serious problems with plausibility and consistency. Kahn et al. (2016) identify completeness, conformance and plausibility as important dimensions of healthcare data quality, which helped me understand why basic profiling alone was insufficient.

My understanding of data cleaning consequently became more critical. Rather than automatically replacing questionable values, I learned to preserve the original information and introduce validation indicators. This allowed records to be investigated without introducing unsupported assumptions. I also developed a better understanding of how data extraction and transformation decisions affect subsequent modelling and visualisation.

The individual implementation reinforced these lessons. I created a relational SQLite database containing nine interconnected tables and used SQL validation to check structural integrity. Although the database retained all 10,000 encounters and returned no foreign-key violations, substantial source-data inconsistencies remained. This distinction between structural correctness and information accuracy was particularly important. A technically successful database does not necessarily provide a trustworthy foundation for decision-making.

Developing an interactive R Shiny dashboard presented another learning opportunity. The visualisations made patterns in hospital activity accessible, but also demonstrated how easily professional-looking outputs could create an impression of reliability. The recorded readmission rate could be displayed accurately as a supplied dataset measure, yet the absence of a defined readmission period limited its interpretation. I became more conscious of the responsibility involved in communicating uncertainty alongside analytical findings.

The collaborative element also contributed to my development. Having responsibility for the AI component required me to produce work that could inform the group's wider database proposal. It highlighted the importance of explaining technical findings clearly enough for other team members to use them. However, my engagement with the module's weekly practical activities was not as consistent as it could have been. Balancing academic work with professional responsibilities meant that some opportunities to consolidate technical knowledge were missed. Looking back, completing smaller exercises more regularly would have helped me develop confidence earlier and reduced my reliance on AI-generated assistance.

Now what? Applying and extending my learning

The module has influenced how I approach data analysis professionally. In my current role, I have developed a Power BI dashboard containing sensitive financial information. When considering how to share it with senior leadership, I recognised that producing useful visualisations was only part of the responsibility. I also needed to consider confidentiality and appropriate access controls, so I retained the dashboard within the organisation's Microsoft environment and arranged controlled access through Microsoft Fabric.

I now place greater emphasis on understanding where data originates, whether it is sufficiently reliable and how its limitations should be communicated. These considerations are relevant to marketing analytics as well as healthcare data, particularly when reports influence business decisions.

My immediate development priority is to strengthen my independent Python and SQL skills. Although AI-assisted code generation has been valuable, I want to become more confident explaining, modifying and debugging code without relying on generated solutions. I also intend to practise data cleaning and validation more systematically, documenting assumptions and retaining evidence of decisions.

For future collaborative projects, I would establish clearer milestones for my individual contribution and engage more consistently with the supporting practical activities. I would also introduce data-quality checks earlier in the process, before substantial effort is invested in database design or dashboard development.

Overall, the most important change in my thinking is that effective data science is not simply about processing information or producing sophisticated outputs. It requires critical judgement about the quality, meaning and responsible use of the data itself.
